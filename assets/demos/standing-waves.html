Why supra-aural headphones can fake a high-frequency notch

The space between the earphone and the eardrum is a short tube. At high frequencies a standing wave forms in it. Change the ear's length and watch what happens to the audiogram of someone with perfectly normal hearing.

3 · The audiogram you would record

True hearing is 0 dB HL at every frequency. Any shift away from 0 comes from the ear's acoustics, not from hearing.

The dip

The standing wave gives every ear a peak and a dip in how much sound reaches the eardrum. Between about 5 and 9 kHz they sit close together, and where they fall depends on the length of the ear. A few millimetres of difference moves a test frequency from the peak to the dip.

The calibration

The audiometer's 0 dB HL is set from the average ear on a coupler. It assumes everyone's ear passes on the average amount of sound. If your dip falls on a test frequency where the average ear isn't dipping, your eardrum receives less sound than the audiometer assumes.

The false loss

Less sound at the eardrum means you need a louder tone, so the threshold looks worse. The result is a notch at 6 or 8 kHz with normal hearing. Short ears tend to lose 6 kHz and long ears 8 kHz. Re-seating the headphones changes the effective length by a few mm, so the notch can move or disappear.

About the model. This is a simplified tube, closed at the eardrum, with a small loss term. It's driven by an earphone that isn't an ideal pressure source: its diaphragm impedance rises with frequency. Voss et al. (2000) measured that real earphones aren't ideal sources. Ear lengths of 22–36 mm (canal plus concha) set the average ear. Real ears add the pinna, cushion leaks and eardrum impedance, which can make individual differences larger than shown here. The shape of the effect is right, but the dB values only illustrate it and aren't predictions for a patient. Further reading: Voss et al.
